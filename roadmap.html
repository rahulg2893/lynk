Product & engineering roadmap, v5.1 · Sep 2026

Lynk Roadmap

A personal chat app for friends and family that remembers the plan. Messaging comes first, and every chat is end-to-end encrypted. Plans, to-dos, memories and search that finds things again are what make it different, and they all run on your own device, so nobody else ever reads your chats.

Positioning

The problem Lynk solves

Group chats are where plans get made, and where they get lost. A group agrees on "dinner Friday 8pm at Nando's". By Thursday it's buried under 200 messages: nobody knows who's coming, nobody put it in a calendar, and someone asks "wait, what time?" three times. The same goes for "can you pick up Mum from the airport", a friend's ramen tip or an address sent once. WhatsApp, iMessage and Telegram are good at moving messages and bad at remembering what they meant.

One line: a private group chat that remembers the plans, so your friends actually show up.

Why it's different

  • It keeps what matters from a chat: plan spotting with RSVPs and a calendar, to-dos and lists, Saved, and Ask Lynk to find things again. Catch up shows what's waiting on you instead of making you scroll.
  • Without reading your chats: every chat is end-to-end encrypted and the smart features run on the phone. Private apps (WhatsApp, Signal) aren't smart about your chats, and apps that are smart usually read them on a server. Doing both is the part that's hard to copy.

The hard truths

  • Nobody switches chat apps for features. People move when their group moves. The pitch has to be strong enough for one person to bring a whole group over.
  • "WhatsApp plus everything" blurs the story. Side chats, voice notes, translation and memories are nice, but they're not the reason to switch. Plans are.
  • Big apps can copy the feature. Apple or Meta could add plan detection. Doing it on the device, under end-to-end encryption, is the defensible part.

Next step: pick one kind of group and one moment and make it great, such as friend groups making weekend plans or families coordinating pickups and school runs. Talk to 10 people in that group before building more features.

Decisions made along the way

  • Personal chat, not a workplace tool (v3 to v4).
  • Removed the prompt.txt effects and Lenis smooth scrolling in favour of a few purposeful animations.
  • Moved from pink and plum to Apple greys with a blue accent.
  • Account screens run on a mock store shaped like the future API (src/lib/account.ts), so wiring the server changes data calls, not pages.
  • The theme switch moved from the app dock into Settings › Appearance, with Match system as the default.
  • One repo for every app: apps/web, apps/mobile, and later apps/server for the Go backend.
  • One Expo app for both phones (v5.1): apps/mobile replaces the planned apps/ios and apps/android. It shares the web's logic and will share the generated protocol, and there is one mobile codebase instead of two.
  • No unread badge on the Chats tab: iOS pins tab badges past the icon's corner, onto the next tab's highlight. The count shows in the Chats header and on Catch up instead.
  • Full motion for everyone: the OS "Reduce motion" setting is deliberately not honoured, by product decision. Revisit before launch (WCAG 2.3.3); recorded in MASTER.md.
  • A red token for destructive actions only: --danger #D70015 (white text 5.38:1) and --danger-ink #FF6961 in dark (6.03:1).
  • "Nearby mode" (chatting with no internet over Bluetooth and Wi-Fi) is possible in the mobile app but deferred until after v1.
  • End-to-end encryption by default (v4.9): every chat, with no opt-out and no server-readable mode. Smart features move onto the device, and the server never reads message content. This replaces the earlier "normal and private chat modes" plan (conflict 1, ADR-09).

Next up: the positioning work above, then the backend half of phase 0. Add apps/server with the Go skeleton and Docker Compose, then connect the existing sign-in screens to real passkeys, Apple and Google.

Conflicts and how they are resolved

A personal app raises different trade-offs than a workplace one. These five decide the shape of v1.

1. Smart features vs end-to-end encryption

People expectPersonal chats to be private, and many expect end-to-end encryption.
Smart features needSomething to read messages to find plans, memories and answers.

Every chat is end-to-end encrypted, and the smart features run on the device. The server relays and stores ciphertext only and can never read a message, a plan or a list. Search, spotting, memories, transcripts and translation run on each member's phone or computer, over messages already decrypted there. Answers and catch-up summaries use an on-device model where the device has one, and fall back to plain results where it doesn't. There is no server-readable mode. The trade-off: answers are weaker on older phones, and nothing can be computed across devices by the server.

2. Memories about other people

Useful"Mei is vegetarian" helps when planning a dinner.
RiskyA fact said in one chat must not surface in another chat Mei is not part of.

Memories live inside the chat they came from. They are visible only to that chat's current members, disappear when the source message is deleted, and the person a memory is about can see and remove it. Nothing is shared across chats in v1.

3. Web first vs phone first

Built so farA web client and landing page.
RealityPersonal chat happens on phones, with push notifications.

Web for the messaging core, then the phone apps immediately after. The protocol is generated for every client, so the phone apps reuse the same server with no API changes. Anyone testing Lynk with friends needs the phone app before the smart features matter. It is one Expo app for iPhone and Android, and its frontend is built.

4. The spec's workplace features

Spec asks forWorkspaces, five roles, audit logs, capsules, a knowledge graph, a conversation map.
Personal chat needsGroups, a few admin controls, and privacy people can understand.

Cut or shrink them. Group admin and member are the only roles, auditing becomes a personal security log, and capsules, the graph and the map wait until real use shows a need. The engineering ideas behind them (permission-aware retrieval, sourced suggestions) stay.

5. Smart features on very different devices

UpsideOn-device models cost Lynk nothing per message, so the free tier stays free and there are no ads.
DownsideA new iPhone can summarise a busy group; an old Android phone or a browser without a built-in model can't.

Rules first, models where available, plain results everywhere else. Search (full-text plus small on-device embeddings), rule-based spotting, memories, transcripts and translation work on every supported device. Answers and catch-up summaries light up where Apple Foundation Models, Gemini Nano or the browser's built-in model are present, and say plainly when they aren't. Each feature degrades to something useful, never to an error.

How it fits together

System architecture

One Go binary in three roles: api (HTTP + WebSocket), worker (push and media) and relay (outbox to NATS). Web and mobile speak the same protocol. Everything a chat says is encrypted on the sending device and decrypted on the receiving ones; the server routes and stores ciphertext.

Send path (never waits for AI)

  1. Client encrypts the message with the chat's current MLS epoch key, pads it to a size bucket, creates an idempotency key and shows it as sending.
  2. Gateway checks membership, blocks and rate limits. It can't read the payload.
  3. One transaction: ciphertext with the next per-chat seq, plus an outbox event.
  4. Ack the sender, then fan out to every member's devices through NATS.
  5. Offline members get a push with an encrypted payload their device decrypts.

On each receiving device

  1. Decrypt, store locally, and add to the on-device search index.
  2. Cheap rules look for times, places, questions to someone, and lists.
  3. Likely candidates go to the on-device model (where present), which suggests a plan, to-do or memory.
  4. A saved suggestion is sent to the chat as an encrypted event, so every member's device has it.
  5. Voice notes are transcribed and messages translated on the device, for members who want it.

PostgreSQL

Schema

The server keeps accounts, keys, memberships and an ordered log of encrypted events per chat. Plans, lists, memories and search indexes are rebuilt on each device from that log, so none of them exist in readable form on the server.

WebSocket

Event protocol

One authenticated socket per device. Every event is safe to process twice.

{
  "v": 1,
  "id": "evt_01J9…",      // unique, used to deduplicate
  "type": "MESSAGE_CREATE",
  "ts": "2026-09-24T14:03:11.402Z",
  "chat_id": "ch_…",
  "side_chat_id": null,
  "seq": 4182,            // per-chat order
  "correlation_id": "req_…",
  "payload": { … }

Event types

MESSAGE_CREATE · MESSAGE_UPDATE · MESSAGE_DELETE · REACTION_ADD · REACTION_REMOVE · TYPING_START · TYPING_STOP · PRESENCE_UPDATE · READ_UPDATE · CHAT_CREATE · CHAT_UPDATE · MEMBER_UPDATE · SIDECHAT_CREATE · PLAN_SUGGESTED · PLAN_SAVED · PLAN_RSVP · TODO_SUGGESTED · TODO_SAVED · TODO_DONE · LIST_UPDATE · MEMORY_SUGGESTED · MEMORY_SAVED · NOTIFICATION_CREATE · SYNC_REQUIRED

Client to server: HELLO with resume cursors, SEND, TYPING, ACK, PING.

Reconnect and ordering

  • HELLO carries the last seq per open chat. The server replays gaps, or sends SYNC_REQUIRED for large gaps.
  • Clients drop duplicate event IDs and buffer events that arrive ahead of a missing seq.
  • Unsent messages stay in the local outbox and retry under the same idempotency key.

Privacy model

Access comes from being in the chat, and encryption enforces it: only current members' devices hold the keys. Smart features follow the same rule because they only ever run on those devices.

Who can see what

  • You can read a chat's messages from your joined_at onward, unless the group admin shares earlier history when adding you.
  • Group admins can rename the group, add or remove people, and change settings. Members can leave at any time.
  • Blocking hides messages, presence and invites in both directions.
  • Plans, to-dos, lists and memories inherit their chat's access. They never appear outside it.

Smart features that cannot leak

  • Everything runs on your device over messages your device could already decrypt, so there is nothing extra to leak.
  • Every membership change is a signed MLS commit that each member's device checks, so the server can't slip in a hidden member. Leaving starts a new epoch you have no keys for; joining late means you only get keys from then on.
  • No message, plan, list, memory, transcript or index is ever sent to a model provider.
  • What the server does see is listed in Encryption, along with how each part is kept to a minimum.
End-to-end encryption

Encryption

Every chat is end-to-end encrypted, always, with no setting to turn it off. This is the full design: the threats it defends against, what is encrypted, what the server can still see, and the limits no messaging app can remove.

Protected against

  • Lynk's servers and anyone who runs, subpoenas or breaks into them: they only ever hold ciphertext.
  • Anyone on the network: TLS outside, MLS inside.
  • A server that tries to add a hidden member: every membership change is a signed MLS commit that each device checks.
  • A server that swaps someone's keys: every key is in a public, append-only transparency log that clients check, and security codes let two people confirm in person.
  • A stolen key later: forward secrecy protects past messages, and regular key updates (at least daily) heal the chat after a compromise.
  • People who left or were removed: they have no keys for anything said afterwards.

Encrypted end to end

  • Messages, edits, deletes, reactions, replies, mentions and side chats.
  • Photos, files and voice notes: each encrypted with its own key, which travels inside the message.
  • Plans, RSVPs, lists, to-dos, memories and saved items: they are events in the chat, not server records.
  • Group names, photos and settings; your name, photo, bio and settings (shared with contacts through a profile key).
  • Typing indicators, read receipts and push previews.
  • Backups, with a key from your passkey (WebAuthn PRF) or a 64-character recovery code.

What the server can still see, and how little

  • Your username (so people can find you) and which devices you have.
  • Which accounts are in which chats, needed to deliver messages.
  • When messages are sent and their size, padded into fixed size buckets so length says little.
  • IP addresses while connected: never written to logs, and rate-limit counters expire within 24 hours.
  • No content in logs, metrics or crash reports, and no third-party analytics or tracking SDKs in the apps.

Closing the side doors

  • Link previews are made by the sender's device and sent encrypted; the server never fetches a link.
  • Invite links carry the group's key material after the #, which browsers never send to the server.
  • Smart features, search, transcripts and translation run only on the device; nothing is sent to a model or speech service.
  • Stored chats are encrypted on the device: iOS Data Protection and Android Keystore with SQLCipher; on the web, a non-extractable WebCrypto key over IndexedDB.
  • New devices get history by device-to-device transfer or an encrypted backup, never from a readable server copy.
  • Abuse reports send only the reported messages, from the reporter's device, with message franking so they can't be forged.

Limits we say out loud

  • Your device and the people you talk to: encryption protects messages in transit and on the server, not on a hacked phone, and anyone in a chat can screenshot or forward what they see. "Delete for everyone" asks devices to delete; it can't undo a screenshot.
  • The web app: a website is downloaded again on each visit, so a compromised server could serve altered code. Reproducible builds, published hashes and Subresource Integrity reduce this; the phone apps give the strongest guarantee.
  • Losing every device and your recovery code loses your history, because nobody else holds a readable copy.
  • Leaving Lynk on purpose: exporting a plan to your calendar, sharing a file to another app or keeping notification previews on the lock screen moves that content outside Lynk's encryption, by your choice.

Background jobs

Flow

  1. Writes add an outbox row in the same transaction.
  2. The relay publishes to JetStream subjects such as lynk.msg.created.
  3. Durable consumers: push, media expiry, notify. None of them can read content.
  4. Jobs are idempotent, keyed by event ID.

When things fail

  • Retries with backoff, then a dead-letter stream and a replay view.
  • Smart features have no server dependency, so they keep working offline and during outages.
  • Messaging never depends on AI, Redis or storage being up.

What could go wrong

Risks

High

Nobody switches chat apps alone

A chat app is worthless until your friends are on it. Win one kind of group with plans first, make inviting a group one tap, and let people still see a plan's link from outside Lynk.

High

Memories about people who never agreed

Keep them scoped to one chat, visible to its members only, removable by the person described, and deleted with their source.

High

Getting the encryption right

Use OpenMLS, never custom crypto; external cryptography review before launch; interop, fuzz and state-machine tests for membership changes, device loss and key rotation; key transparency and security codes so a swapped key can't go unnoticed.

High

Encryption on the web

A web app is re-downloaded from the server on every visit, so a compromised server could serve bad code. Pin and publish build hashes, use Subresource Integrity, and recommend the phone apps for people who need the strongest guarantee.

High

Lost devices, lost history

With no server copy, losing every device loses the chats. Device linking, and encrypted backups keyed from the passkey or a recovery code, set up during sign-up.

Med

Spam and abuse without reading messages

Invite-based contact, rate limits on new accounts, blocking, and reporting that sends the reported messages from the reporter's device (with message franking so reports can't be forged). Required for the app stores anyway.

Med

Uneven smart features across devices

Rules and search work everywhere; model features light up where an on-device model exists and say so plainly where it doesn't.

Med

Prompt injection in messages

Messages are treated as untrusted data by the on-device model. It only suggests; every field is validated before saving, and it can never take actions.

Low

Wrong suggestions

Nothing is saved without a tap, and dismissing is one tap too.
